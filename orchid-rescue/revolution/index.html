Revolution

Revolution is one of the longest-serving orchids in my collection. I have photos of her going back to at least 2020, which means she has survived several years of my orchid education, two interstate moves, and whatever other indignities come with being one of my plants.

Revolution originally belonged to a friend who had received her as a gift. Like many gift orchids, she was appreciated while the flowers were open. Once the blooms began dropping, the enthusiasm disappeared.

I had more interest in what happened next, so Revolution came home with me.

She was not one of the dramatic, nearly dead clearance-rack rescues. She was simply an orchid that someone no longer wanted once it stopped looking like the orchid pictured on the tag. I took over from there.

That turned into a much longer commitment than either of us probably expected.

Revolution started with me in Texas and later made the move to Florida. A year after that, she made the entire trip back to Texas.

That is a lot of travel for a plant whose preferred transportation method is generally “remain attached to a tree.”

She handled both moves and continued growing. The older photos show the different homes and growing conditions she passed through along the way, making Revolution one of the better records of how my plant collection—and my ability to care for it—has changed over time.

At the beginning of 2026, Revolution was preparing a particularly good show. One established flower spike was developing buds while a second spike was beginning near the base of the plant. The first white flowers opened in February, followed by a full display that continued through March and into April.

The blooms were large, white, and arranged across two spikes. It was one of the strongest bloom cycles I had seen from her.

While she was flowering, the pot was clearly becoming crowded. The roots were packed tightly into old, moisture-retentive material, but the plant was still healthy enough that I decided to wait until the flowers finished before disturbing it.

On June 13, after the bloom cycle was over, I finally repotted Revolution. Inside the root mass, I discovered that the original nursery plug was still there.

Revolution had been carrying that compact plug since before she came to me—through years of growth, the move from Texas to Florida, and the move back to Texas. It had probably been buried inside the pot for most of the time I owned her.

I removed the plug and repotted the plant into a fresh, open bark mix with better airflow around the roots. For a plant that had been living with that hidden problem for years, Revolution handled the change remarkably well.

The weeks after the repot were not dramatic, which was exactly what I wanted. Revolution settled into the new bark and began directing her energy into a strong new leaf. Through July, that leaf continued to lengthen and unfold while the mature leaves remained thick and firm. Her oldest bottom leaf began slowly yellowing as the plant reclaimed its stored nutrients, but there were no signs of a setback from the repot.

Revolution has been through more than most orchids I own. She survived being abandoned after blooming, two interstate moves, and years with a nursery plug hiding in her roots. At this point, she has earned her place in the collection.

Now she gets to do the boring but important work: growing roots, making leaves, and eventually deciding when she is ready to put on another show.
